Travel Planner — privacy policy

Travel Planner has no account, and your trips and saved places stay on your device. To draw maps and find places, it asks MapTiler and a few public services directly from your device, never through us. What does come to us is an anonymous usage count, once a day, which you can turn off.

What this covers

This policy is about the Travel Planner app — the iOS build distributed through TestFlight, the Android build and the web version. The site you are reading it on is covered separately by Legal & privacy.

Travel Planner is an independent project. There is no company backend, no sign-up and no user account. “We” below means the developer of the app.

What we store

Nothing of yours. Everything the app keeps lives on your device: your trips and their plans, the places you saved, the posts read from a data export you imported (their links, captions, authors and hashtags), your settings, and a cache of place lookups so they are not repeated. In the installed app that is the app’s own private storage; on the web it is your browser’s storage.

A data export from Instagram, TikTok or YouTube is read on the device and never uploaded. Only your saved posts are taken from it; nothing else in it is kept. Titles the app looks up for TikTok and YouTube videos are used once, to find the place, and are not kept.

If you add an Anthropic API key, it is kept apart from everything else: in the iOS Keychain or the Android Keystore (on the web, in your browser’s storage).

What we do keep are the running totals of the usage statistics below, which hold nothing per device.

What leaves your device

To do its job the app asks outside services for map and place data. Each request goes from your device straight to the service — which therefore sees your IP address — and that service’s own privacy policy applies to it:

  • MapTiler (maps and place search over OpenStreetMap data, under our MapTiler account): the area of the map you look at; the places you search for; the destination you type; when you plan a town outside the built-in set, the town’s position, to find places to eat near it; and, during an import with online lookup on, the place names found in your posts’ captions (and coordinates a caption gives), to find where they are. MapTiler handles these requests for us and may keep them in its logs under its privacy policy. The app names itself to MapTiler and Wikimedia, with a link to its page.
  • Wikipedia and Wikidata: a location, to find the sights and towns around it.
  • TikTok and YouTube: when a saved video has no title in your export, its address, sent to that platform’s official oEmbed service, for the title. The title is used once, on your device, to find the place, and is not kept. Instagram is never contacted: captions come only from your export.
  • Anthropic — only if you added your own key and agreed to this in the app: the trip’s destination, the interests you chose, and the names and categories of the planned places (places from your imported posts included), to write the day summaries and tips. No posts, captions or your location are sent. The request is made with your key, under your agreement with Anthropic.

Look up places online, in Settings and on the Import screen, turns the import’s online lookups off; the import then works offline. Links you tap — directions, ticket searches, a post, a Wikipedia article — open in your maps app or browser.

Apart from those, the app sends one request a day to us: the anonymous usage count described under Usage statistics, while that is switched on.

YouTube

Travel Planner uses YouTube API Services. When a YouTube video among your saved posts has no title in the export, the app asks YouTube for it, sending only the video’s address. No Google account is used. The title and the channel name are used once, on your device, to find the place the video is about, and are not stored: the app keeps only the video’s link from your export and the place it found.

By using Travel Planner you agree to be bound by the YouTube Terms of Service.

Google handles those requests under the Google Privacy Policy.

Open-source software

The app is built with open-source packages; their licences are listed in the app under Settings → Open-source licences. Maps and place search © MapTiler; map data © OpenStreetMap contributors (ODbL).

Usage statistics

To see how many people use Travel Planner and which parts are worth more work, the app keeps a few counters on your device and sends them in one request a day: the first time you open it that day, or, if that request doesn’t get through, the next time you open it or come back to it. Never more than one a day. It is on unless you turn it off, and the app says so in a one-line notice that stays until you dismiss it. Versions without the switch described below send nothing. The request carries exactly this:

  • the app’s name, the platform (iOS, Android or web) and the app version;
  • the language the app is shown in;
  • your device’s date that day — the date, not the time;
  • the week you started using a version with statistics, or “earlier” if you used Travel Planner before they existed;
  • whether this is the first request ever, the first of the week and the first of the month;
  • how many times each of a fixed set of actions happened since the last request — a trip planned, an export imported, a screen opened;
  • how many errors of each kind happened, by type alone — for example TypeError, or “a place lookup failed”.

It never carries an identifier of any kind — no device, user, advertising or session ID — and none of your trips, destinations, places, imported posts or anything else you type, no error messages and no location. Nothing in it says at what time anything happened.

The request goes to a small function of ours on Vercel, which adds the counts to running totals in an Upstash Redis database: per app, per day, week and month, nothing per device or per request. The totals are deleted after 400 days. The function sees your IP address, as any server does, and never stores or logs it; to stop one address flooding it, it holds a scrambled, one-way form of the address in memory for ten minutes. Vercel keeps a log of the requests it serves, your IP address among them, for about an hour, under Vercel’s privacy policy.

The numbers are not used for advertising or to track you, not combined with any other data, and not shared or sold. There is no crash reporting of our own beyond these counts.

Turning it off: Settings → Share anonymous usage statistics. Switching it off stops the requests and deletes the counters kept on your device. In a browser that sends Global Privacy Control, the web version starts with it off and shows no notice, since there is nothing to tell.

Deleting your data

Settings → Delete all data removes every trip, saved place, imported post and the API key from the device; the key also has a delete button of its own. Deleting the app removes everything. The usage totals hold nothing per device, so there is no entry of yours in them to delete; they expire after 400 days.

Beta testing

While the app is distributed through TestFlight, Apple collects the installation and crash data described in Apple’s TestFlight privacy notice. That is Apple’s collection under Apple’s terms. It reaches us only as anonymous crash reports and the feedback you choose to send, and never includes your trips, your saved places or anything you imported.

Children

The app is not directed at children and collects no personal data from anyone, of any age: the usage counts above carry no identifiers and nothing you type.

Changes

If this policy changes, the date at the top of the page changes with it, and anything newly sent anywhere is described here before it ships.
