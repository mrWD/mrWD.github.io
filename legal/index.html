Privacy

No cookies, no third-party scripts, no profiles. Nothing here follows you between sites or builds a picture of you. At most two things are stored on your device, both in local storage: your light/dark theme preference, so the page doesn't flash the wrong colour on your next visit, and — only if you arrived through a link inside one of my apps — a note to hide donation buttons for the next twelve hours, because App Store rules don't let an app lead to a payment page. Neither ever leaves your browser, and clearing your browsing data removes both.

One thing is counted. When you click a button that goes to a product — Open, Download, Add to Chrome, Ask for an invite — your browser sends one request carrying the name of that button (for example film-table--open) and no cookie or identifier. It goes to a small counter of my own, running on Vercel, which adds one to a public tally on Abacus, a free counter service. Only the button name is passed on; Abacus never sees you. What's kept is the tally — how many times each button was pressed, not who pressed it. Block the request and the button still works; the click simply isn't counted. Links to the source code and to this site's own pages are not counted at all.

Like any server you connect to, the counter does see your IP address. Vercel keeps a log of the requests it serves, your IP address among them, for about an hour, under Vercel's privacy policy. The counter itself writes nothing down: to keep one machine from inflating the numbers, it holds a scrambled, one-way form of your address in memory for a minute or so, and never stores it anywhere.

The site is served as static files by GitHub Pages. Like any web host, GitHub processes connection data such as your IP address in order to deliver the page; that's covered by GitHub's privacy statement.

The projects themselves are separate programs with their own behaviour. The trackers keep your library on your device. Six apps send me anonymous usage counts, described in the next section. The browser extensions are additionally covered by the privacy disclosures in their store listings. Where a project sends anything to a third party, its own page and repository say so.

Usage statistics in the apps

FilmTable, GamesTable, BooksTable, GoalsTable, InvestTable and Travel Planner send me anonymous usage counts, so I can see how many people use them and which parts are worth more work. It is on unless you turn it off, and the app says so in a one-line notice that stays until you dismiss it. The switch, Share anonymous usage statistics, is under Profile in FilmTable, GamesTable, BooksTable and GoalsTable, under Settings in Travel Planner, and at the bottom of the page in InvestTable. Versions of the apps without that switch send nothing.

The app keeps a few counters on your device and sends them in one request a day: the first time you open it that day, or, if that request doesn’t get through, the next time you open it or come back to it. Never more than one a day. The request carries exactly this:

  • which app it is, the platform (iOS, Android or web) and the app version;
  • the language the app is shown in;
  • your device’s date that day — the date, not the time;
  • the week you started using the app with statistics in it, or “earlier” if you used it before they existed;
  • whether this is the app’s first request ever, the first of the week and the first of the month;
  • how many times each of a fixed set of things happened since the last request — a title added, an import finished, a screen opened;
  • how many errors of each kind happened, by type alone — for example TypeError, or “the TMDB lookup failed”.

It never carries an identifier of any kind — no device, user, advertising or session ID — and nothing you keep in the app: no titles, goals, figures, trips or places, no searches, nothing you type, no error messages and no location. Nothing in it says at what time anything happened.

The request goes to a small function of my own on Vercel. It adds the counts to running totals in an Upstash Redis database — per app, per day, week and month, such as how many devices opened an app on a given day, on which platforms and in which languages. Nothing is kept per device or per request, and the totals are deleted after 400 days.

Like any server, the function sees your IP address; it never stores or logs it. To stop one address flooding it, it holds a scrambled, one-way form of the address in memory for ten minutes. Vercel keeps a log of the requests it serves, your IP address among them, for about an hour, under Vercel's privacy policy. Upstash receives only the totals, from the function, never from you.

The numbers are for me, to decide what to work on. They are not used for advertising or to track you, not combined with any other data, and not shared or sold.

Turning it off stops the requests and deletes the counters kept on your device. In a browser that sends Global Privacy Control, the web versions start with it off and show no notice, since there is nothing to tell. Blocking the request also works: the app behaves the same without it.
